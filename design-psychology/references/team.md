# The team and the organization

What ships depends on the room as much as on the design file. For solo makers, many of these traps play out inside one head: sunk cost, plan loyalty, rebuild urges, metric chasing.

## 31. Seniority sways judgment (authority bias)
- What happens: A senior person's doubt or excitement shifts the work without any new evidence. People start bringing safer, more agreeable evidence upward. Clients and founders count too.
- Ask: "What's behind that?" Ask early, before anyone starts changing things. A reason (user data, a constraint, market context) is something to work with. If no real reason surfaces but everyone adjusts anyway, it was the title talking.

## 32. Meetings breed false agreement (conformity, group polarization, groupthink)
- What happens: People tone down objections to match the mood. Discussion tends to push a group further in the direction it already leaned, which feels like clarity but isn't. The resulting consensus then gets mistaken for proof.
- Do: Everyone writes their reaction privately for 2 minutes before discussion. Record the objections that lost in the decision log so future reviews can see them.

## 33. Rebuilds look cheaper than they are (planning fallacy, not-invented-here, UX debt)
- What happens: The imagined new version hasn't met real constraints yet, so it looks easy. The current one looks worse partly because someone else built it. Most mess comes from many small additions, and the forces that created it will still be there after a rebuild.
- Do: Before a rebuild, list 5 concrete, evidenced problems ("4 in 10 new users quit at the permissions step," not "feels dated") that share one root cause only a rebuild can fix. If you can't reach 5, fix the worst problem, measure, and repeat.

## 34. Targets drift away from people (Goodhart's law, Campbell's law)
- What happens: Once a number becomes a goal, effort bends toward moving the number rather than helping people. A healthy dashboard creates calm while users quietly work around problems.
- Ask: "How could we hit this number while making the product worse?" before it becomes a target. Keep testing and reading reviews and support emails while the numbers look good. App examples: session length, notification opt-in rate, trial starts, a rating prompt timed to inflate stars.

## 35. Promises outrank new evidence (escalation of commitment)
- What happens: Announced dates and scope start to weigh more than what was learned since. Questioning them feels disloyal, so the conversation moves from whether to build it to how to deliver it on time.
- Sounds like: "It's already in the release notes." "Sales already announced it."
- Ask, in a session that isn't a status update: "If this weren't planned yet, would we add it now, given what we've learned?"

## 36. Past effort keeps bad work alive (sunk cost fallacy)
- What happens: After months of work, cutting a feature feels like admitting waste, so it gets propped up with labels, tooltips, and extra onboarding instead.
- Ask: "If a stranger handed us this feature today, would we release it as is?" Effort already spent is the team's cost. Users should not pay for it with a broken feature.

## 37. Lessons leave with people (organizational memory)
- What happens: The reasoning behind past decisions walks out when people leave. Documents keep what was decided but not why, so old debates resurface as if new.
- Do: Before reopening a decision, find out whether it was tried and how it went. Keep a decision log with the reason and the losing objections. A design system records craft choices, not why a pattern was dropped.

## 38. The brief's framing can be wrong (problem framing)
- What happens: How a problem is worded decides where you look. "Users don't tap Upgrade" leads to button styling. "Users don't yet believe the app is worth paying for" leads to the free experience. Some problems shift once touched: reducing drop-off can surface a support or retention problem.
- Do: Before designing, ask: What evidence shows there's a problem? Who has it? What happens if we do nothing? Is this someone's preferred solution dressed up as a problem? Reframe at least once (symptom vs cause). Consider the environment the product has to live in, not only the product.

## 39. Research as cover (motivated reasoning)
- What happens: Once a direction is chosen, research gets recruited, worded, and summarized in its favor. The study asks how to build it, never whether to.
- Do: Before a study, write the specific result that would cancel or change the project. If you can't, the study is there to justify a decision, not test it. Useful research surprises you now and then.
