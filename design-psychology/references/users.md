# Users

People arrive with goals, habits, feelings, culture, and limited attention. Their real context matters more than their answers in an interview.

## 21. Outcomes over steps (goal-setting, self-determination, jobs to be done)
- What happens: People use a product to change something in their life (sleep better, keep a baby's feeding times straight, stop forgetting bills). Screens and steps are only the vehicle. People also want to feel in charge, capable, and connected. A high completion rate can hide that the life change never happened.
- Do: Before mapping a flow, write the user's real outcome in one concrete sentence. Check each step: does it move toward that sentence, or mostly serve the product? Measure whether people got the outcome, not only whether they finished.

## 22. Effort now outweighs payoff later (present bias)
- What happens: A cost today feels heavier than a benefit next week. Five small setup screens look harmless in a flow diagram but feel like one big demand to a new user.
- Look for: Signup wall, paywall, permission prompts, tour, and preferences, all before any value.
- Do: Find the moment a new user first feels the app help them. Move it earlier: try before an account, one real result before asking for details, permission prompts at the moment they are needed, paywall after value is felt. A finished profile or setup is preparation, not value.

## 23. Promises aren't behavior (intention-behavior gap, social desirability)
- What happens: People sincerely plan to do things and then don't, because daily life brings fatigue, routines, and other demands. In research they also present the person they aspire to be.
- Do: Treat stated intent as a weak signal. Test with a small real cost (leave an email, import data, return 3 days in a row, pay). Before building for a stated need, list what could get in the way. Ask how they handle it today and what happened the last time they tried to fix it. Build for the distracted, low-energy version of the user.

## 24. Emotion arrives before reasons (affective primacy)
- What happens: People feel something about a product before they understand it, and build their reasons afterwards. A small negative flinch (salesy, patronizing, stiff, clingy, intrusive) shortens their patience for everything that follows.
- Look for: Eager helpers, assistants that pop up before any trust exists, chirpy error messages, guilt-trip copy, interruptions in the first seconds.
- Do: Ask testers for one word describing their gut reaction, then which moment caused it. Trust facial expressions and hesitations over the tidy explanation at the end. Fix a bad first reaction before launch; explaining it away later rarely works.

## 25. Change registers as loss (loss aversion, status quo bias, reactance)
- What happens: Existing users weigh a redesign against what disappeared, not on its own merits. The old version has habit and speed on its side, and removing things without consent provokes pushback.
- Do: Loss audit before shipping: list what users rely on without thinking (positions, gestures, shortcuts, names) and what the change does to each. Heavy losses need a heavy gain people feel quickly. Otherwise phase it in, announce it, keep the old path for a while, and allow switching back. Treat the backlash as information, not irrationality.

## 26. Taste is cultural (cultural cognition)
- What happens: A sparse layout that looks refined in one market can look bare or untrustworthy in another. Density can signal capability (super apps). Much UX research comes from a narrow Western sample.
- Do: Before entering a market you did not design for, briefly show the main screen to about 5 locals and ask whether anything feels missing, rather than whether they like it.

## 27. The current habit is the competitor (endowment effect, habit loops)
- What happens: People overvalue what they already use, and routines fire from context cues without a fresh decision. The competitor is often Notes, a spreadsheet, screenshots, chat, or muscle memory, not another app.
- Do: Observe the current routine: which app they reach for, which workaround keeps coming back, what moment sets it off. Lower switching cost: import, carry over what works. Win one recurring moment of the day before asking for a full switch. Hook into existing cues (widgets, share sheet, Shortcuts, a reminder at the moment of the old habit).

## 28. Each option is a decision (feature fatigue, Hick's law, choice overload)
- What happens: Long feature lists help a product look capable before purchase and slow people down after. Most features see little use but still add scanning and second-guessing. A busy screen signals effort before anyone starts. Power features are fine once users grow into them, just not on day one.
- Do: Options audit. One obvious default. Keep advanced controls off the main path until the user needs them.

## 29. Attention is scarce (limited attention, banner blindness)
- What happens: Users arrive in the middle of something else with whatever focus is left. Areas that look like ads or noise get skipped on reflex. Turning up the volume trains people to tune it out sooner.
- Do: Before any modal, push notification, badge, or banner, ask what the person was doing and whether this is worth breaking into it. If people skip something, ask whether it deserved attention instead of making it louder. Never optimize for attention at the user's expense.

## 30. Dead ends breed giving up (learned helplessness)
- What happens: Failing repeatedly with no visible way out makes people stop trying, even when they badly need the result, and they expect the next attempt to fail too. Quitting is the natural response. A sense of control has to be designed in.
- Do: First failure test before release. Every broken state needs a concrete action available now: retry that keeps input, resume where they left off, a direct support link, a plain explanation of what happened and whose side the problem is on. Design unhappy paths before polishing the happy path.

## 40. Persuasion vs manipulation (ethics)
- What happens: The same psychology can serve people or exploit them. The component is identical; what differs is the intent and whether it tells the truth.
- Check: Would a user who saw exactly how this works feel looked after, or played? Could they explain what they just agreed to?
- Never: pre-ticked consent or marketing, fake urgency or countdowns, fake progress, cancel harder than signup, guilt-trip copy, accept-all in one tap while decline is buried, a free tier made painful so people upgrade without really choosing.
- Fine: defaults that match what most users want, honest progress, a reassuring undo, reminders that serve the user's own goal.
