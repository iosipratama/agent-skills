# The designer's mind

The designer's own head bends the work before any user sees it. These biases feel like confidence, so knowing about them does not switch them off. Each one needs an outside check. They apply to the agent too: an AI read of a screen is still one viewpoint.

## 1. Treating yourself as the user (false consensus)
- Trap: Your own preferences feel normal, so people who struggle get written off as unusual.
- Sounds like: "Most people would get this." "That tester was an outlier."
- Check: For each choice, ask what it rests on: something you saw users do, or what you would want? If you hesitate, it is your taste. Ask: could a newcomer get this on first sight, with nobody explaining?
- Rule: A user who struggles tells you about the design, not about the user.

## 2. Fear trims the idea (impostor feelings, self-handicapping)
- Trap: Worry about looking naive makes you dilute a strong direction before anyone pushes back. A safer pattern here, a softer label there, an extra line of explanation. Each step seems sensible, so nobody notices the idea getting weaker.
- Sounds like: "Too risky for v1." "Let's play it safe." Better explorations left in a hidden page of the file.
- Check: Do I have evidence against it, or am I just nervous about defending it? Name what you are protecting yourself from. Present the bold option next to the safe one and let it be judged.

## 3. Critique feels personal (cognitive dissonance, ego)
- Trap: When the work stands in for your competence, bad results get rationalized. Skilled people come up with more persuasive rationalizations.
- Sounds like: "That tester was in a hurry." "The test setup was off." "Real users will figure it out."
- Check: Pre-mortem: assume it flopped and list the causes. Ask someone to spend 10 minutes only hunting for weak spots. Note the very first excuse that pops into your head. That reflex shows where you are attached.

## 4. Familiar passes for clear (processing fluency, illusion of understanding)
- Trap: After days in the file, the screen is easy to read because you know it, not because it is clear. You feel you understand a flow until you try to explain each step.
- Check: "Is this clear, or am I just used to it?" Talk through the screen as a newcomer: where am I, what's my first move, what will this tap do. The first fuzzy moment points at a design problem. Take a break, come back, and note where you are filling gaps from memory.

## 5. Repetition inflates liking (mere exposure vs real taste)
- Trap: Looking at your own design over and over makes you like it more, even when nothing improved. Real taste comes from seeing lots of good work and holds up across very different products.
- Check: Show it to a fresh person for about 30 seconds and ask what it is for and what grabs them first. Lay it beside 3 strong products tackling a similar job.

## 6. Waiting for inspiration (creativity comes from making)
- Trap: Rereading the brief and collecting references while the canvas stays empty. Ideas show up while reacting to rough work.
- Check: Set a 20-minute timer and make rough, probably wrong options. A concrete bad draft tells you more than an idea still in your head. Resisting the timer is a sign of stalling. Feed yourself inputs from outside design too.

## 7. Pressure shrinks thinking (stress, threat rigidity)
- Trap: Near a deadline the decision collapses to ship or slip. Teams repeat old moves, decisions move up the chain, and the people who know the risk stop speaking.
- Check: When a worry surfaces late, ask "What's the smallest check that would settle this, and how long would it take?" That turns a vague worry into a sized task, often small enough to do before launch. Notice who went quiet.

## 8. Anchored on the first sketch (design fixation, Einstellung effect)
- Trap: Most later "exploration" just rearranges the first idea. Defining a product as a better version of a competitor hands the competitor your design decisions. Even knowing an example is flawed does not stop you inheriting its assumptions.
- Check: Name the one assumption the current idea takes for granted (it's a list, it's started manually, it needs its own screen, it needs an account). Make one rough version where it is false. Flip it, don't tweak it.

## 9. Expertise hides the gaps (curse of knowledge)
- Trap: What you know fills holes in the interface automatically. Reviewers who were in the kickoff miss what newcomers stumble on. Being told about this bias barely helps.
- Check: Put it in front of someone with no context and keep quiet. Note every pause, misreading, and abandoned attempt. Each marks a gap your own knowledge was filling in.

## 10. Filtering for agreement (confirmation bias)
- Trap: Supportive comments get recorded in full while awkward ones shrink to a two-word label. Failures get renamed as copy, education, or discoverability problems.
- Sounds like: "Users just need a tooltip." "It's a copy problem." Curious language sounds different: "What were they expecting?" "Where did it go off track?"
- Check: Decide in advance which result would mean you are wrong. Assign someone to make the case against shipping. When reviewing notes, start with whatever surprised you.
